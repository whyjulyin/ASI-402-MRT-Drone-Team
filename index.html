    01 Project Overview

    Introduction

    The University Rover Challenge (URC) is a robotics competition for university students, organized annually by the Mars Society near the Mars Desert Research Station (MDRS) in the desert of southern Utah, United States. The competition tasks student teams with designing and building next-generation Mars exploration rovers capable of executing field missions that simulate real planetary surface operations.

    Field performance is evaluated across 4 distinct missions: Delivery, Autonomy, Equipment Servicing, and Astrobiology. Each mission is worth 100 points, with a maximum achievable score of 400 mission points. The use of a drone is permitted in the Delivery and Autonomy missions. Overall team standings combine field mission points with preliminary evaluations from the System Acceptance Review (SAR), which is submitted prior to the field competition.

    Problem Statement

    For this competition, our mars rover traverses a large competition field spanning up to 2km with undulating terrain. When direct line-of-sight with the Command and Control (C2) station is broken, it loses its RF link for telemetry and video. The team requires an aerial solution for reconnaissance and to assists in maintaining rover connection in Non-visual Line-of-Sight (NVLOS) situations.

    Proposed Solution

    A quadcopter that hovers above obstructions as an airborne repeater, giving a NVLOS relay, and carries a gimballed camera to scout drive paths, find scattered equipment and spot elevated ArUco markers.

    URC2027 Missions

    Design requirements and constraints

    The 1:1 dummy-mass rule was a key design constraint as each gram of battery adds two grams to the final take-off mass.

    Final Design Assessment

    Why a quadcopter design?

    Evaluating against coaxial and hexacopter layouts, the quadcopter has the lowest structural mass, the fewest mechanical failure points in fine desert dust, and high hover efficiency under the mass rule.

    Literature Review - URC2026 Drone Team

    03 Concept

    Iterative Design Loop

    Our drone sizing was iterative; every change in component or battery mass alters take-off mass, required thrust, power and endurance. We ran the loop until the mass budget closed.

    Additional Concept study: 3 kg or 5 kg?

    We compared between three concepts: two lighter designs that require less battery capacity, with our heavier design. Under the dummy-mass rule the battery competes with the fixed mission kit for every gram. We used this approach to evaluate the effects of increasing the drone's battery capacity and its impact on performance.

    • A · 3 kg, today’s parts. Only 352 g remains for the battery: 10.6 min of hover.
    • B · 3 kg, resized airframe. A lighter airframe frees 659 g: 17.4 min.
    • C · 5 kg design, selected. Carries 1,240 g of battery and hovers 21.7 min, with 136 g of margin under the cap.

    04 Propulsion and energy

    Propulsion Sizing

    The aircraft spends most of its mission hovering as a relay, so the propulsion system is chosen for hover efficiency first and for thrust reserve in desert wind second.

    1Preliminary sizing

    Thover = mg / N Tmax = (T/W) · Thover A = mg / DL D = √(4A / Nπ)

    At 5 kg each of the four motors must lift 1,250 gf. A thrust-to-weight target of 2 asks for 2,500 gf per motor, and a disk loading of 75 N/m² needs 0.654 m² of rotor disk: the 18-inch propeller class.

    2Motor and propeller

    Two shortlisted pairs were compared at the 1,250 gf hover point by interpolating manufacturer test tables. The MAD 5008 draws about 10% less power, delivers 11% more thrust per watt and saves 192 g across four motors. Its lower peak thrust still clears the 2,500 gf requirement.

    The two tables come from different manufacturers’ test set-ups, so the comparison stays provisional until our own thrust-stand runs.

    Propeller size

    The manufacturer tested three Fluxer Pro sizes on this motor. The 17-inch needs the same hover power but has less thrust in reserve. The 19-inch saves 3 W yet adds only 3% thrust and demands a larger, heavier frame. The 18×6.1 is the compromise, and it matches the disk-loading estimate.

    3Battery Selection

    Because the dummy mass must match the battery weight, a heavier pack adds energy but also twice its own mass to lift. This graph recalculates the final flight endurance, taking into account changes in take-off mass, thrust, motor operating point and power for each battery mass.

    Graphical Analysis

    • Endurance rises across the whole feasible range, with diminishing returns.
    • The curve only peaks near 2.3 kg of battery, where battery mass equals the rest of the aircraft, as momentum theory predicts.
    • The 5 kg cap binds long before that, so battery mass is maximised within it.

    A high-energy lithium-ion pack was preferred over a high-discharge LiPo as it provides more energy per gram, which suits a hover mission, provided the pack can supply the required current. Hover draws about 20 A for propulsion against a 48 A continuous rating.

    We will verify the thrust and electrical power on a thrust stand, then replace the hover-only model with a mission profile covering take-off, climb, transit, survey, hover and descent.

    Frame Specifications

    06 Electrical and control

    Wiring Architecture

    The architecture separates propulsion power, regulated electronics power and data, so that a fault or noise on one cannot take down another.

    Why DShot, not PWM

    DShot is digital. Every ESC channel reads the same checksummed throttle value, one of 2,000 steps, so all four motors respond alike and no throttle calibration is needed. That steadies hover in gusty wind.

    Bidirectional DShot also returns motor RPM to the Pixhawk, for vibration filtering and motor-health checks.

    Flight-controller choice

    Six controllers were screened against two gates: redundant inertial sensors and redundant power inputs. The Pixhawk 6X on the Mini Baseboard gave the best sensor isolation and expansion for 58 g.

          07 Communications

          Radio Frequency (RF) Links

          This section details the design of the NVLOS communications link between the rover, base station and drone, the architecture for live video feed and radio control. We split the communication systems into three independent wireless links for each function. This allows us to design and evaluate the control, video and rover telemetry separately within their own frequency band, accounting for latency and reliability.

          Link budget

          A link budget adds transmit power and antenna gains, subtracts free-space path loss (FSPL) and other losses, and compares the result with receiver sensitivity. We size each hop for 1.2 km, a conservative figure for the 1 km expected, and require a 20 dB margin.

          FSPL = 32.44 + 20 log10 fMHz + 20 log10 dkm PRX = PTX + GTX + GRX − FSPL − L Margin = PRX − sensitivity

          2.4GHz Control

          35.4 dB at 2.4 GHz and 50.9 dB at 900 MHz. Either band meets the target with room to spare.

          2.4GHz Relay

          23.4 dB per hop, the same in both directions.

          5.8GHz Video is the limiting link

          13.7 dB with the stock antenna. An 8 dBi directional antenna raises it to 19.7 dB; The stock antenna's performance will be confirmed in our range test before purchase.

          Control and relay both use 2.4 GHz. We plan to place them on channels at opposite ends of the band and verify this by test. Singapore’s IMDA restricts 900 MHz relays locally, and other rover radios may share that band, so its use will be settled in testing.

          08 Testing

          Timeline for Testing

          Throughout this first semester, our team established our preliminary system architecture and component selections, conducting RF link tests to evaluate the feasibility of the proposed design. Our next step is to conduct individual component level tests to validate our design parameters.

            Preliminary 2.4 GHz repeater compatibility test

            Purpose

            The on-board GL.iNet repeater and the base-station Rocket M2 use different Wi-Fi configurations. Before building around these systems we checked that they are compatible.

            Method

            We joined the repeater to the Rocket M2 network, stood the two devices 10–15 m apart and pinged in each direction, in both extender and router modes.

            Result

            The first attempts timed out: the devices sat on different IP subnets. After the repeater was readdressed onto the Rocket M2 subnet, router mode returned all four packets in 2–30 ms. Extender mode still failed.

            Analysis

            Early pings succeeded in one direction only, which points to the repeater’s firewall rejecting unknown addresses. Next we will disable the firewall and repeat the test, then measure packet loss and throughput at range and across the full base–drone–rover chain.

            End of Semester Field Testing

            Our preliminary testing has shown individual connection between the components are feasible. Our following field tests conducted in reading week will provide more comprehensive performance data to verify both maximum link range and end-to-end connectivity.

            During this time, we also plan to simultaneously conduct battery performance validation, and motor-thrust testing before we proceed with the preliminary assembly of the drone itself.

            09 Technical Challenges, risk management and plan

            Technical Challenges & Timeline

            During our first semester, we encountered two main technical challenges. We managed these challenges through additional margins in our preliminary calculations, and plan to verify our solutions through further testing.

            01 Challenge

            Piloting from inside the base station

            Operators sit in an enclosed base station that blocks radio. A 20 m LMR400 coaxial cable carries the control signal to an antenna outside, and an HDMI cable brings the goggles’ video in.

            Both control bands keep well over 20 dB after the cable. We found no reliable loss data for the HDMI run, and adaptors add loss of their own.

            Validation plan

            • Measure the cable’s insertion loss.
            • Verify control-link performance with the extension fitted.
            • Test HDMI video stability at the required resolution and frame rate.

            02 Challenge

            Designing from datasheet values

            Budget and time allow one prototype, so our figures rest on supplier data: frame dimensions, part masses, motor efficiency, battery voltage sag and radio transmit power. Errors compound, because an under-estimated battery mass also raises the dummy mass.

            Margins built into every estimate

              Conservative figures let the other subsystems plan missions inside limits we expect to meet.

                10 Conclusion

                Conclusion

                Our project aims to develop a custom quadcopter for supporting the NUS Mars Rover Team in the University Rover Challenge 2027, with our main capabilities of aerial reconnaissance and establishing NVLOS communications relay. Overall, we laid the foundation of our design in this semester through an iterative design process, and developed a clear plan for full integration and operational testing of the drone in semester 2.

                Throughout this first semester, our team established our preliminary system architecture and component selections, conducting subsystem-level analysis to evaluate the feasibility of the proposed design. Significant progress was made in propulsion and battery optimisation, mechanical design, electrical system integration and wireless communication architecture. Using iterative mass budgeting, flight endurance estimation, and link budgeting calculations, we developed a design that both achieves mission objectives and stays within competition restrictions. Initial wireless repeater connectivity has also been successfully established.

                Nevertheless, uncertainties remain in terms of actual flight performance, due to a heavy reliance on manufacturer specifications and theoretical calculation frameworks. While conservative design margins have been incorporated to account for our potential discrepancies, further experimental validation required to verify actual performance and reliability. In semester 2, we aim to complete mechanical and electrical integration of the parts, along with individual component testing, before proceeding with flight tests, CV integration and ultimately mission planning for the final competition. These activities will help to address our uncertainties in flight performance and evaluate the system against our initial design requirements, to ensure an operationally ready drone before our final submission and URC2027 Competition.
